Wallet Styles We Produce

Bifold

The default men's wallet: two vertical card columns, a full-width note compartment, optional ID window. Folds once. Around 11 × 9.5 cm closed. Highest-volume construction and the most forgiving to manufacture, which is why it carries the lowest entry price.

Trifold

Two folds, more card capacity in a shorter footprint. More panels, more skiving and more stitching than a bifold, so it sits slightly higher in cost. Popular in the US market.

Cardholder

Minimal — typically 3–6 card slots, no note compartment, sometimes a central pull-through pocket. Least material and least labour, so the cheapest to make, and increasingly the strongest seller in the under-30 demographic. A good first product for a brand testing the market.

Money clip

Card slots with a sprung steel clip on the spine or exterior. The clip is bought-in hardware, so the cost sits between a cardholder and a bifold depending on clip quality and whether it is branded.

Zip-around and travel wallet

Larger format with a perimeter zip, passport slot and note sleeves. The zip is the cost driver — a YKK Excella against a generic zip is a real difference in both cost and perceived quality.

"Genuine leather" is a grade, not a compliment

It is the single most misleading term in the industry. Genuine leather is real leather from the lower layers of the hide after the valuable top grain has been split away — weaker, and it does not patina. If a competing quote is dramatically cheaper than ours, this is the first thing to check. We do not produce bonded leather at all.

For wallets specifically we recommend full-grain in 1.0–1.4mm. Thinner than that and the wallet feels flimsy; thicker and it will not fold cleanly or sit in a pocket. Available finishes across our hide range: {{pg:list-hide-types}}.

Which finish for which brand

  • Aniline or pull-up — heritage and patina-focused brands. Natural markings visible; the leather lightens where it flexes.
  • Smooth — formal and corporate. Even, clean, predictable.
  • Pebble or embossed — fashion. Textured grain hides wear well over time.
  • Veg tan — craft and tooling brands. Darkens dramatically with use.

Hardware & Interior Customisation

  • Card slot count and layout — the main driver of cutting and stitching labour.
  • ID window — clear PVC or a cut-out; adds a component and an assembly step.
  • RFID-blocking layer — a shielding interlayer laminated into the construction. Adds material cost and one lamination step; strong selling point in US and EU markets.
  • Lining — self-lined leather (premium), pigskin (traditional and breathable), or fabric (cost-effective).
  • Thread colour and stitch density — tonal or contrast; higher stitch counts read as more premium and cost slightly more.
  • Edge finish — painted, burnished or raw. Edge finish is the fastest way to tell a cheap wallet from a good one, and it is worth paying for.

Branding: Embossing, Debossing & Packaging

Embossing and debossing

Both press your logo into the leather using a heated metal die. Debossing pushes the mark in; embossing raises it. A die is a one-off tooling cost on the first order and is then reused indefinitely — so it is cheap amortised across repeat orders and disproportionately expensive on a very small first run. Blind (uncoloured), foil and pigment fills are all available.

Labels and inserts

Woven or printed labels stitched into the interior, plus care cards and authenticity inserts. Low cost, high effect on perceived quality.

Packaging

Options run from a simple polybag through kraft sleeves and rigid gift boxes to full retail cartons with barcodes. For Amazon sellers, packaging must also satisfy FBA prep rules — polybag suffocation warnings, FNSKU placement and carton labelling. That is covered fully in our FBA sourcing guide.

Construction Details That Separate Good Wallets from Cheap Ones

A wallet is a simple object, which means there is nowhere for poor work to hide. Four details do most of the work:

  • Skiving. Leather is thinned at fold lines and seams so the folded wallet stays slim. An unskived wallet is visibly bulky and will not close flat. This is the clearest single indicator of whether a factory knows what it is doing.
  • Edge treatment. Painted edges applied in multiple thin coats and sanded between them, or burnished edges on veg tan. Cheap wallets get one thick coat that chips within months.
  • Stitch density and backstitching. 7–9 stitches per inch reads as considered. Backstitching at pocket mouths — the highest-stress points — prevents the unravelling that ends a wallet's life.
  • Card pocket tension. Slots must hold a card securely when new and still accept one after a year of stretching. This comes from pattern tolerances, not from material, and it is where mass-produced wallets most often disappoint.

When you receive a sample from us or from anyone else, check those four things before anything else. They predict long-term customer satisfaction better than the leather grade does.

Yes. A shielding layer is laminated into the construction during assembly. It adds modest material and labour cost and is a strong selling point in US and EU markets.

Yes, once. The die is a one-off tooling cost on your first order and is then stored and reused on every repeat order at no further charge.

Yes — most of our wallet work is client-designed. Send a tech pack, or a physical reference product and we will pattern from it. We also offer design support if you have a concept but no technical drawing.

What leather do you recommend for wallets?

Full-grain cowhide in 1.0–1.4mm. Thinner feels flimsy, thicker will not fold cleanly or sit comfortably in a pocket. Choose the finish based on whether your brand story is patina (aniline, pull-up) or consistency (smooth, pebble).
