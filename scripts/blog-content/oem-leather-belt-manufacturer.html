Constructions

  • Single-ply full-grain — one solid strap cut from a heavy hide, edges finished. The most durable and the most honest construction. Uses 3.0–4.0mm leather.
  • Bonded two-ply — two lighter layers laminated and stitched, often with a contrasting reverse. Allows a two-colour belt and a lighter feel.
  • Reversible — two-ply with a rotating buckle so one belt serves black and brown. Popular in gifting; the buckle mechanism is the cost driver.
  • Braided / woven — strips woven into a stretchable strap with no fixed holes. Labour-intensive, sits at the top of the belt price range.
  • Stitched-edge dress belt — a stitch line inset from the edge. Adds perceived quality at modest cost and is the single best value upgrade on a dress belt.

Leather for Belts

Belts are a structural product. The leather is under constant tension and repeated flexing at the same points, so material choice is less forgiving than on a wallet.

  • Full-grain cowhide, 3.0–4.0mm — the correct answer for a quality belt. Strong enough not to stretch or crack at the holes.
  • Vegetable-tanned — firmer, tools and burnishes beautifully, and darkens with wear. The choice for heritage and craft brands.
  • Chrome-tanned — more flexible, wider colour range, more economical. Right for most fashion belts.
  • Buffalo — pronounced natural grain and excellent tear strength for rugged casual belts.
The belt to avoid making

Bonded leather belts — leather fibre bonded to a backing — are common at the bottom of the market and they fail predictably: the surface cracks and peels within a year, usually at the buckle fold. They generate returns and one-star reviews that outlast the saving. We do not produce them. If a competing quote is dramatically below the range on this page, bonded material is the most likely explanation.

Available finishes across our hide range: {{pg:list-hide-types}}. For belts, smooth and pebble finishes wear best; heavy pull-up finishes will lighten noticeably at the fold points, which some brands want and others do not.

Buckle Sourcing & Custom Hardware

The buckle is the most visible component and typically the largest single component cost in a belt.

Stock buckles

We hold a working range of pin, plate, box-frame and reversible buckles in antique brass, nickel, gunmetal and matte black. No tooling cost, no lead time, and appropriate for most orders. This is where a first order should start.

Custom buckles

A branded buckle — your logo cast, engraved or etched into the plate — requires a one-off mould or die. Three things to understand before committing:

  1. Tooling is a one-time cost

    Paid on the first order, then stored and reused indefinitely at no further charge. It is expensive amortised over a small first run and negligible over repeat orders.

  2. Tooling adds lead time

    Budget an extra 15–25 days on the first order for mould production and buckle sampling. This is in addition to belt sampling, not concurrent with it.

  3. Material matters more than finish

    Solid brass and stainless outlast plated zinc alloy substantially. Plated zinc looks identical on day one and wears through at the contact points within a year. Specify the base metal, not just the finish.

Buckle attachment

  • Snap studs — buckle is interchangeable by the wearer. Popular for gift sets where one strap ships with two buckles.
  • Riveted — permanent, cleanest look, strongest.
  • Stitched loop — traditional dress belt construction.

Embossing, Debossing & Print

Belts have limited visible surface, so branding placement is a real design decision rather than an afterthought.

  • Debossed logo on the reverse near the tip — the standard, visible when the belt is off or the tip flips.
  • Embossed keeper loop — logo on the loop that holds the tail. Subtle and premium.
  • Engraved or cast buckle — the highest-impact option, and the one requiring tooling.
  • All-over embossing — a pattern pressed across the full strap. Distinctive; requires a full-width roller die.
  • Edge paint in a brand colour — an underused and inexpensive way to make a belt recognisably yours.
  • Foil or pigment fill — gold, silver or coloured fill in a debossed mark.

Sizing and packaging

Belts are sized by waist, not by total strap length, and the industry convention is that the middle hole corresponds to the stated size. Standard runs are 30–44 inches in even increments. Cut-to-size belts — supplied long with the buckle detachable so the retailer or customer trims — reduce your SKU count substantially and are worth considering for a first range.

Packaging options run from a rolled polybag through kraft sleeves and gift boxes to full retail hanging cards with barcodes. For Amazon sellers, prep requirements are covered in our FBA sourcing guide.

Can you make belts with my own buckle design?

What leather thickness should a belt be?

3.0–4.0mm full-grain cowhide for a quality belt. Thinner leather stretches under tension and cracks at the holes. Vegetable-tanned is firmer and burnishes well; chrome-tanned is more flexible with a wider colour range.

By waist size, with the middle hole corresponding to the stated size. Standard runs are 30–44 inches in even increments. Cut-to-size belts — supplied long with a detachable buckle — cut your SKU count substantially.

Do you make reversible belts?

Yes. Two-ply construction with a rotating buckle, typically black on one face and brown on the other. The rotating buckle mechanism is the main cost driver over a standard belt.

Yes, and it is our most common first order. Belts and wallets in the same leather and edge-paint colour make a strong gift set, and combining them lets you meet both minimums in a single shipment.
