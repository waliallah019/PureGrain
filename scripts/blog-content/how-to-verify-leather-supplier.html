We are a leather supplier publishing a guide on how to check up on leather suppliers. That is a slightly odd thing to do, so here is the reasoning: buyers who verify properly place better orders, complain less, and stay longer. Suppliers who dislike being checked are usually the ones you should not be using. Apply everything below to us.

The five steps

  • 1. Verify the company legally exists and has actually exported before.
  • 2. Order a paid sample — it tests the product and the relationship at once.
  • 3. Verify certifications with the issuing body, not from a PDF.
  • 4. Audit — a live unscripted video call is the minimum.
  • 5. Place a small trial order at real commercial terms before scaling.

Why This Matters More in Leather Than Most Categories

Three things make leather unusually easy to get wrong:

  • The grading vocabulary is actively misleading. "Genuine leather" sounds like a quality claim and is in fact a grade denoting the lower hide layers after the valuable top grain has been split away.
  • Defects surface late. Poor tanning or a weak finish can look perfect on delivery and fail six months into customer use — by which time you have sold the whole batch.
  • Batch-to-batch variance is inherent. Leather is a natural material. The question is never "does it vary" but "does the supplier grade and document, or just hope".

Step 1 — Check Registration & Export Licences

Cheap, fast, and it eliminates a meaningful share of enquiries.

  • Registered company name and number. In Pakistan that means an NTN (National Tax Number) and SECP registration where applicable. Every country has an equivalent. Ask for it in writing.
  • Chamber of Commerce membership — chambers issue the Certificates of Origin that exports depend on, so membership is a functional requirement, not a decoration.
  • Industry association membership — in Pakistan, the Pakistan Tanners Association (PTA) for tanning businesses.
  • Evidence of prior export. Ask to see a redacted bill of lading or export declaration. A supplier who has genuinely exported can produce one in minutes; one who has not will change the subject.
  • Bank account in the company name. A request to pay a personal account, or an account in a different country from the business, should end the conversation.
A single question that sorts most of this

"Can you send a redacted copy of a recent bill of lading for a shipment to my region?" It is a normal, professional request. The answer — its speed, its specificity, and whether the destination matches the markets they claimed to serve — tells you more than any amount of website copy.

Step 2 — Request and Evaluate Samples

Pay for the sample. Free samples select for suppliers with a marketing budget, not suppliers with good production. Most manufacturers, ourselves included, credit the sample cost against a first production order — which achieves the same thing without the adverse selection.

What to inspect when it arrives

  • Cut edge. Look at the raw edge of the leather. Full-grain shows a dense fibre structure through the thickness. Bonded leather shows a visible backing layer — this is the single most reliable physical tell.
  • Grain surface. Real full-grain has irregular, non-repeating pores. A perfectly uniform repeating pattern means an embossed correction over a lesser grade.
  • Edge finish. Painted or burnished edges, evenly applied, no bubbling or gaps. This is where cost-cutting shows first.
  • Stitching. Consistent length, straight lines, backstitching at stress points, no skipped stitches, thread ends properly finished.
  • Hardware. Zips should run smoothly under slight tension. Check whether load-bearing fittings are solid metal or plated zinc alloy.
  • Smell. Real leather smells of leather. A strong chemical or plastic smell suggests heavy synthetic coating or poor finishing chemistry.
  • Flex test. Fold the leather firmly. Full-grain creases and recovers. A finish that cracks or flakes at the fold will fail in customer use.

Test the relationship, not just the product

The sample stage is also a behavioural test, and it is the cheapest one you will get:

  • Did they ask clarifying questions, or just say yes to everything? A supplier who agrees to every request without qualification is not reading your specification.
  • Did the sample match what was agreed?
  • How did they respond when you asked for a change?
  • Did they hit the timeline they gave, and tell you proactively if not?

Step 3 — Verify Certifications

Certificates are easy to fake and easy to misrepresent. Verification is straightforward if you do two things: get the number, and check what the certificate actually covers.

Three claims that should stop you

"We are CPSIA certified." No factory is. CPSIA compliance is demonstrated by third-party testing on a specific production batch, in your name. The same is true of California Proposition 65.

"We are CE certified" (for motorcycle apparel). CE marking under EN 17092 is issued against a specific garment construction by a notified body — not as a blanket factory certificate.

A certificate with no number, no issuing body, or an expired date. Check the date. Expired certificates get reused for years.

Also check scope. An ISO 9001 certificate covering "trading and export of leather goods" is not the same as one covering manufacturing. The scope line is printed on the certificate and is frequently where the real story is.

Step 4 — Conduct a Factory Audit

Virtual audit — the minimum

Request an unscheduled live video call from the production floor. Not a pre-recorded video, not a photo gallery — a live call where you direct what you see.

  • Ask them to walk from cutting through stitching to QC and packing, without cutting the call.
  • Ask to see your product in production if the timing allows.
  • Watch for scale mismatch: does the floor plausibly support the capacity claimed?
  • Ask to see the QC station and the archived reference samples.
  • Ask an unplanned question — "what is behind that door?" A rehearsed tour handles a script; it does not handle improvisation.

Third-party audit — for larger commitments

SGS, Intertek, Bureau Veritas and TÜV all conduct factory audits in Pakistan and every other sourcing origin. Worth the cost once your annual commitment justifies it. Social compliance audits (Sedex/SMETA, BSCI) matter separately if you sell to retailers who require them.

The trading company question

Ask directly: "Do you own this production unit, or do you subcontract?" Neither answer is disqualifying — a good export house coordinating specialist units often delivers better results than a single factory pretending to do everything. What matters is that you know, because it determines who is accountable when a batch is wrong. Evasion on this question is the red flag, not the subcontracting itself.

Step 5 — Start with a Trial Order

Place a small order at real commercial terms. Not a sample — a genuine production run, invoiced, shipped and documented.

This is the only test that exercises the whole system: production scheduling, batch consistency across multiple units, QC, packing, export documentation, freight coordination, and how they behave if something goes wrong. A supplier can produce one excellent sample by hand. Fifty consistent units is a different capability.

What to check on the trial shipment

  • Consistency across units — line them up. Colour, dimensions, stitch quality, edge finish should match unit to unit, not just match the sample individually.
  • Documentation accuracy — invoice, packing list and certificate of origin should match the goods exactly. Errors here cause customs delays and suggest weak process control.
  • Packing quality — did it survive transit intact?
  • Timeline adherence — did they ship when they said?
  • Communication under pressure — if something slipped, did they tell you before you asked?

Red Flags to Watch For

  • Prices far below every other quote. Usually a different material — bonded or split leather quoted against your full-grain specification.
  • "Yes" to every question. Real manufacturers push back on impossible tolerances and unrealistic timelines. Universal agreement means nobody has read the spec.
  • Reluctance to sample. There is no legitimate reason to resist a paid sample.
  • 100% payment up front. Industry standard is roughly 30% deposit and 70% against a copy bill of lading.
  • Payment to a personal or third-country account. Stop.
  • Stock photos. Reverse image search their product photography. Images lifted from other suppliers are common and disqualifying.
  • No physical address, or an address that is a residence.
  • Vague on certifications — "we have all certificates" without numbers.
  • Pressure to decide quickly. Manufacturing capacity is real and sometimes genuinely limited, but urgency used as a closing tactic is a sales technique, not a production constraint.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if leather is real from a sample?

Check the cut edge — real leather shows a dense fibre structure through its thickness, while bonded leather shows a distinct backing layer. Check the grain for irregular, non-repeating pores. Fold it firmly: full-grain creases and recovers, poor finishes crack or flake.

Should I pay for samples?

Yes. Free samples select for suppliers with marketing budgets rather than good production. Most manufacturers credit the sample cost against your first production order, which removes the objection.

How do I verify an ISO certificate?

Get the certificate number and the issuing certification body, then contact that body directly — most maintain online verification. Also read the scope line: a certificate covering "trading and export" is not the same as one covering manufacturing.

Is it a problem if my supplier subcontracts production?

Not inherently — export houses coordinating specialist units often outperform a single factory claiming to do everything. The problem is not being told. Ask directly, and treat evasion rather than subcontracting as the red flag.

What payment terms are safe?

Roughly 30% deposit against a confirmed purchase order and 70% against a copy bill of lading, so the balance is paid only once goods are demonstrably shipped. Letters of credit give more protection on larger orders. Never pay 100% up front, and never to a personal account.

Do I need a third-party inspection?

For a first order of meaningful value, a pre-shipment inspection from SGS, Intertek or Bureau Veritas is inexpensive relative to the risk. Once a supplier has delivered consistently across several orders, most buyers reduce inspection frequency.

How small should a trial order be?

As small as the supplier's minimum allows while still being a genuine production run rather than a sample. Fifty units tests batch consistency in a way five never will. Ours start at {{pg:moq-lowest}} pieces specifically so this test is affordable.
